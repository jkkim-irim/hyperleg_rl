Summary

This paper extends the authors’ previously developed
HyperLeg active-toe mechanism to a 14-DOF biped robot and
compares toe-equipped and toe-ablation configurations under
a common reinforcement-learning procedure. The simulation
incorporates cooperative-actuation mapping, motor
torque–speed limits, joint friction, and motor-side energy
consumption. Straight-line walking and a T-shaped
directional-change task are evaluated. The authors report
that the toe-equipped configuration reduces CoT and GRF by
17.5% and 5.0%, respectively, and reduces average and
maximum path deviation by 25.0% and 34.0%. The main
contribution is the quantitative evaluation of an
active-toe biped using an actuator- and transmission-aware
learning framework, although several claims currently
extend beyond what the experiments directly demonstrate.

Strengths

2.1 Clear Quantitative Comparison

The paper presents a clear comparative study by applying
the previously developed HyperLeg mechanism to a full biped
platform and evaluating toe-equipped and toe-ablation
configurations using multiple quantitative metrics. Rather
than reporting only successful locomotion, the manuscript
compares power consumption, CoT, GRF, task-completion time,
speed, and path deviation. This multi-metric evaluation
provides a useful basis for examining both the benefits and
limitations of the active-toe configuration.

2.2 Cooperative-Actuation-Aware Modeling

The manuscript does not simplify the coupled knee–ankle–toe
mechanism as ideal direct-joint actuation. Instead, joint
velocities and torques are mapped between joint and motor
spaces through the cooperative-actuation Jacobian, and the
motor commands are constrained by four-quadrant
torque–speed envelopes. This is a technically meaningful
treatment of the proposed transmission structure.

2.3 Direct Consideration of Energy Consumption

The use of Joule heating and signed mechanical power is
better aligned with the energy-efficiency objective than a
conventional squared-joint-torque penalty. This formulation
may also help reveal how the active-toe configuration
redistributes energy demand across the hip, knee, and ankle
joints.

2.4 Presentation of Absolute and Relative Performance

Tables 3 and 4 report both absolute values and relative
changes, making the effects of the toe-equipped
configuration easy to interpret. The joint-level power
breakdown in Fig. 6 also provides more insight than the
whole-body CoT value alone.

Major Revisions

3.1 The Scope of the Agility Claim Exceeds the Evaluated
Metrics

The title and conclusion use the broad terms “agility” and
“turning agility.” However, the experiment is limited to a
single T-shaped course, and the main improved metrics are
average and maximum path deviation.

According to Table 4, the toe-equipped model has lower peak
and average speeds than the toe-ablation model. The
completion times are nearly identical, whereas the path
deviations are smaller for the toe-equipped configuration.
Therefore, the results directly support the following
conclusion:

Under the evaluated T-shaped course and training
conditions, the toe-equipped configuration exhibited lower
path deviation together with slightly lower movement speed.

The current evidence is more consistent with improved
path-tracking accuracy under a possible speed–accuracy
trade-off than with a general improvement in agility or
faster turning. The smaller path deviation may result from
improved turning dynamics, but it may also reflect a more
conservative and slower policy.

The statement “This confirms that active toes improve
turning agility” should therefore be revised to a more
evidence-based statement, such as “The toe-equipped
configuration showed reduced path deviation under the
evaluated directional-change task.”

3.2 The Physical Mechanism Behind the Directional-Change
Result Is Not Analyzed

The toe joint primarily produces sagittal-plane motion. It
is therefore not immediately evident why this actuation
should improve yaw-direction turning or path tracking. The
manuscript reports reduced path deviation but does not
identify the mechanical mechanism responsible for this
result.

A comprehensive analysis of all possible factors is
unnecessary, but one or two directly relevant quantities
should be reported. Suitable examples include toe-contact
duration, center-of-pressure trajectory, stance-foot slip
distance, lateral GRF, yaw-rate tracking error, or torso
angular-velocity variation.

Without such analysis, the conclusion should remain limited
to the observed reduction in path deviation rather than
attributing the result directly to improved turning
capability.

3.3 The Reported CoT Value Cannot Be Reproduced from the
Stated Quantities

The Introduction states that a 70 kg adult consuming 125 W
at 1.33 m/s has a CoT of 0.316. However, using Eq. (5), the
corresponding CoT is approximately 0.138.

The manuscript should clarify whether 125 W represents
metabolic power, mechanical power, or another quantity, and
explain how the value of 0.316 was obtained.

Table 1 also provides separate motoring and regeneration
efficiencies, whereas Eq. (5) uses signed mechanical power
without clearly describing how regeneration is incorporated
into battery-side power. The treatment of regenerative
power and the meaning of “Mechanical loss” in Table 3
should also be clarified.

3.4 The GRF Metric Is Inconsistently Defined

The abstract reports a 5.0% reduction in “heel-strike GRF,”
whereas Table 3 labels the corresponding quantity as “Avg
GRF.” These terms do not describe the same metric.

The manuscript should specify whether the value represents
vertical GRF or the three-dimensional resultant force,
whether it is a peak or averaged value, whether the
averaging interval covers heel strike or the entire stance
phase, whether the value represents one foot or both feet,
how heel strike is detected, and whether force filtering is
applied.

Until the metric is clearly defined, the reported 5.0%
reduction cannot be interpreted unambiguously as improved
impact absorption. The abstract, main text, and Table 3
should use the same terminology.

3.5 The High-Fidelity and Sim-to-Real Claims Should Be More
Precisely Framed

The simulation improvements described in Section III
primarily concern coupled-transmission mapping, motor
torque–speed limits, joint friction, and motor-side power
estimation.

The manuscript does not provide quantitative hardware
validation of link inertial parameters, belt elasticity,
structural compliance, backlash, foot–ground contact
stiffness, contact damping, or actuator response.
Therefore, the demonstrated contribution is more accurately
characterized as actuator- and transmission-aware
simulation rather than a validated high-fidelity model of
the complete robot dynamics.

The authors should either provide evidence supporting the
high-fidelity claim or use more precise wording. For
example, “high-fidelity simulation” could be replaced by
“actuator- and transmission-aware simulation,” “reduces the
sim-to-real gap” by “is intended to support future
sim-to-real transfer,” and “a rigorous foundation for
closing the sim-to-real gap” by “a simulation-based
evaluation prior to hardware deployment.”

The conclusion should clearly distinguish simulation
observations from performance experimentally validated on
the physical robot.

3.6 The Mechanical-Design Section Should Place Greater
Emphasis on the Active Toe

The mechanical-design section devotes considerable space to
conventional hip, knee, and ankle actuator structures,
whereas the active-toe mechanism, which is the central
subject of the comparative study, is described relatively
briefly and is largely deferred to the previous work [20].

The manuscript need not repeat all details from [20], but
it should provide sufficient toe-specific information to
interpret the present experiments. Relevant information
includes toe length, mass, range of motion, joint-axis
location, contact-surface geometry, torque capacity,
contribution of the toe motor to ankle and knee torque,
posture dependence of the effective transmission ratio, and
the elements inherited from or modified relative to [20].

Generic actuator descriptions could be condensed to provide
more space for explaining how toe geometry and transmission
are expected to affect energy consumption, impact response,
and path-tracking performance.

3.7 Experimental Variability Should Be Reported

The manuscript states that ten trials were conducted for
the walking and directional-change experiments, but Tables
3 and 4 report only mean values. It is also unclear whether
the ten trials represent repeated evaluations of one
trained policy or independently trained policies with
different random seeds.

The authors should report the number of independent
training seeds, explain how the ten trials were generated,
provide the standard deviation or range, and define the
meaning of the shaded regions in Fig. 6.

If only one policy was trained for each configuration, this
limitation should be acknowledged. Terms such as
“significant” should not be used unless supported by an
appropriate analysis of variability.

Minor Revisions

4.1 Academic Writing and Terminology

Expressions such as “rigorously isolate,” “ensuring an
unbiased setup,” “confirms,” and “significantly improves”
are stronger than the current evidence supports. More
neutral expressions such as “compare,” “using the same
training procedure,” “suggests,” or “showed a reduction
under the evaluated conditions” should be used.

The manuscript should use “biped robot” and “bipedal robot”
consistently throughout.

Frequently used abbreviations, including RL, CoT, and GRF,
should be defined at their first occurrence. There is no
need to introduce abbreviations for terms used only once.

4.2 Equations and Notation

The power-consumption reward in Table 2 refers to Eq. (6),
although the manuscript contains only Eqs. (1)–(5). The
correct equation number should be provided.

The current-position term appears to be missing from the
goal-arrival reward equation in Table 2. The equation
should be checked and corrected.

The meanings and values of v_m, d*, σ_arr, [x]+, and
τ_cont,i should be provided. Although the motor safety
threshold is described as heuristically tuned, its
motor-specific values should also be reported for
reproducibility.

In Table 1, the joint ω_max values are identical to
the motor ω_0 values. The authors should verify
whether this row represents motor speed or post-reduction
joint speed and correct the label or values accordingly.

The unit and derivation of C_p should be provided.

4.3 Formatting and Visual Materials

The caption of Fig. 6 should state whether the shaded
regions represent standard deviation, standard error, or a
value range. Fig. 7 should include clear axis labels,
units, and identification of the reference path and robot
trajectories so that the figure can be understood
independently from the main text.

4.4 Reference

Reference [24] does not appear to be cited in the main
text. It should either be cited in an appropriate location
or removed from the reference list.
